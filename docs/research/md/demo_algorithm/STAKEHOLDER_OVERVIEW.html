Stable Preference Mixer · v1

A calmer, explainable way to choose the room’s sound.

CoSound’s pilot algorithm turns recent listener activity, saved-sound tags, and votes on exact mixes into a stable two-to-three-layer soundscape. It is deliberately simple enough to explain today—and instrumented to create better evidence for future machine-learning work.

The current algorithm

Score every valid mix. Choose the best reachable one.

This is an explainable heuristic, not an ML or RL model. It works with CoSound’s existing Player, Sound, Listener, Vote, Prediction, and Cosound data rather than introducing a second product model.

1

Read the room

Use the player’s assigned library and listeners who voted there recently.

2

Build options

Enumerate every valid layer combination with fixed, balanced gains.

3

Estimate fit

Combine a weak tag-based prior with votes on each exact mix.

4

Score the group

Average people equally, then apply a small disagreement penalty.

5

Apply guardrails

Honor the hold time and allow no more than one layer edit.

6

Play and record

Create an exposure record and link later feedback to what played.

Individual preference estimate

Saved sounds contribute weak evidence through their tags. A direct vote only affects the complete mix that received the vote—not every sound inside it.

listener score = (positive votes + 2 × weak tag prior) ÷ (all votes + 2)

With no evidence, a mix begins neutral at 0.50. One downvote on that exact mix lowers it to about 0.33; an untried mix remains at 0.50.

Group decision

Each active listener gets the same social weight. More history may sharpen an estimate, but it never gives someone more control over the room.

group score = average listener score − 0.25 × disagreement

Ties are deterministic: retain the current valid mix first, then prefer fewer layers, then use a canonical mix key.

Fixed v1 Gains are balanced at 1 ÷ √layers—approximately 0.707 per layer for two sounds and 0.577 for three. The scheduler cadence is 30 seconds, transitions allow at most one layer edit, saved-tag prior strength is 0.25, direct-vote smoothing strength is 2, and score ties use eight-decimal precision. These mechanics currently require a design/code change rather than a runtime setting. A 17-sound library has 816 possible two-to-three-layer mixes; only one-edit-reachable options are considered during a transition.

Learning value

The pilot produces a traceable feedback chain.

The implementation records more than “a vote happened.” It preserves which decision was made, how likely the chosen action was, which mix the player received, and how confidently a later vote can be attributed.

Decision

Why this mix?

Inputs, versions, scores, constraints, prior mix, chosen layers, and a plain-language explanation.

Probability

How was it chosen?

The selected action’s probability and the non-zero action set, including exploration when enabled.

Exposure

What was sent?

Command time, exact gains, player acknowledgement, estimated audible time, end time, and status.

Feedback

What did people say?

The listener, raw vote, time, player context, exact exposure link, and attribution quality.

Observed local behavior

A player-acknowledged vote changed the ranking as designed.

In the local test, one downvote on the exact three-layer exposure moved its neutral estimate from 0.50 to approximately 0.33. An untried mix remained neutral at 0.50 and became preferable. This also exposed why the demo should use a two-layer minimum: the old compatible configuration could choose a one-layer alternative.

Interpretation: the feedback loop works, but one vote is intentionally influential in a cold-start demo. More evidence will stabilize relative rankings.

What this enables later: supervised preference benchmarks and carefully scoped contextual-bandit evaluation within actions the pilot actually covered. The logs do not automatically make the system “RL-ready,” nor do they support unbiased claims about actions that had zero probability.

Known limitations

Useful for a demo, with boundaries we should keep visible.

The algorithm is intentionally narrow. These are the main product and evidence-quality limitations to carry into pilot decisions.

Immediate pilot risk: the vote page loads the displayed player layers once and can become stale while the physical player continues to update. A visitor could think they are voting on the old display while the server attributes the vote to the current exposure. Fix or explicitly refresh this view before gathering stakeholder-facing pilot data.
01

Activity is not presence

A vote in the last five minutes is a useful proxy, but it does not prove the person is still physically in the room.

02

Saved sounds are a snapshot

The system sees someone’s current collection, not when each sound was saved or removed.

03

Acknowledgement is estimated playback

The player can confirm receipt and queueing, but not prove that a human heard the exact mix acoustically.

04

No learned sound compatibility

The selector understands tags and exact-mix votes, not whether two recordings blend well in the acoustic or musical sense.

05

Forced novelty has a tradeoff

A finite maximum stay guarantees motion, but can replace a mix people still like. The trace labels those forced changes explicitly.

06

Exploration is initially limited

With exploration off, data is safer for the demo but less useful for estimating what unchosen alternatives would have done.

Next steps

Move from “locally working” to a credible controlled pilot.

The sequence below protects the visitor experience first, then improves evidence quality, then creates the conditions for future learned approaches.

Synchronize the vote page

Refresh the displayed layers when the live exposure changes and make the exposure being voted on explicit. This is the main data-integrity and user-trust gate.

Before pilot

Run the agreed demo profile

Restart the scheduler and worker with the stable predictor, 2–3 layers, a 120-second hold, a 300–600-second maximum stay, and exploration off. Confirm the loaded settings in the recorded trace.

Before pilot

Complete a short staged soak

Observe changes, votes, acknowledgements, and fallbacks over several hours. Verify that no normal decision drops below two layers and that forced changes replace only one layer.

Pilot prep

Add a decision review view

Give operators a simple way to inspect “why this mix,” current exposure status, attribution quality, and recent outcomes without opening a database shell.

Pilot

Set data governance

Agree on retention, pseudonymization, consent language, access, and export format before treating live traces as a reusable training dataset.

Pilot

Benchmark before learning online

After enough attributed feedback, compare simple offline models against this transparent baseline. Introduce limited exploration only with an explicit experiment and safety review.

After data

Recommended decision: approve a controlled local or staging demo after the vote-page synchronization fix and a configuration-verified soak test. Keep the random predictor available as rollback.
