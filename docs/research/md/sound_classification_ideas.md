The classification of soundscapes is a multidisciplinary field, drawing from ecology, acoustic engineering, and sociology. The most widely recognized frameworks come from the work of **R. Murray Schafer** and the **ISO 12913** standards.

Here are the primary ways soundscapes are classified:

---

## 1. Ecological Classifications (The "P-B-A" Model)

Used primarily in acoustic ecology, this system categorizes sounds based on their biological or physical origin.

* **Geophony:** Non-biological natural sounds. This includes the movement of water, wind in trees, thunder, and seismic activity.
* **Biophony:** Sounds created by non-human organisms. Examples include birdsong, insect chirps, and whale vocalizations.
* **Anthrophony:** Sounds generated by humans. This is further divided into:
* **Controlled:** Music, language, and intentional signals.
* **Uncontrolled:** Mechanical noise, traffic, and industrial hum.

---

## 2. Descriptive Classifications (Schafer’s Framework)

R. Murray Schafer, the pioneer of soundscape studies, categorized sounds based on their function and "weight" within an environment.

| Classification | Definition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Keynote Sounds** | Background sounds that are often not consciously heard but outline the character of a place. | The sound of the sea in a coastal town; traffic hum in a city. |
| **Sound Signals** | Foreground sounds that are listened to consciously and often convey information. | A police siren, a church bell, or a whistle. |
| **Soundmarks** | A community sound which is unique or possesses qualities which make it specially regarded by that community. | A specific historic clock tower chime or a unique local foghorn. |

---

## 3. Perceptual Classifications (ISO 12913)

The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) focuses on how humans *perceive* and *feel* about an acoustic environment. This is often mapped onto a two-axis coordinate system:

* **Pleasantness (Horizontal Axis):** Ranging from "Unpleasant" to "Pleasant."
* **Eventfulness (Vertical Axis):** Ranging from "Uneventful" to "Eventful."

Based on these axes, soundscapes fall into four quadrants:

1. **Calm:** Pleasant and Uneventful (e.g., a quiet library or a still forest).
2. **Exciting:** Pleasant and Eventful (e.g., a festival or a busy market).
3. **Chaotic:** Unpleasant and Eventful (e.g., a construction site or heavy traffic).
4. **Monotonous:** Unpleasant and Uneventful (e.g., a constant low-frequency industrial drone).

---

## 4. Hi-Fi vs. Lo-Fi Soundscapes

This classification refers to the **signal-to-noise ratio** of an environment and how much detail a listener can perceive.

* **Hi-Fi (High Fidelity):** Environments where discrete sounds can be heard clearly without being masked by background noise. In a Hi-Fi soundscape, the "perspective" is deep; you can hear a bird in the distance and a footstep nearby simultaneously.
* **Lo-Fi (Low Fidelity):** Environments where individual sounds are masked by a dense "wall of sound." Overlapping signals (usually urban or industrial noise) result in a lack of acoustic clarity.
